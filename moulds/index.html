Kenner

Cincinnati, Ohio · 1947 – 2000

The company that turned licenses into legends. From a toy oven that actually baked to an empty box that launched Star Wars, Kenner spent five decades deciding what a generation would find under the tree.

Origins

Named after a street. Built to last.

In 1947, three brothers, Albert, Philip and Joseph Steiner, started a toy company in Cincinnati, Ohio, and named it after Kenner Street, where their office stood. It was a small beginning for a firm that would end up shaping the shelves of nearly every toy shop in the Western world.

Their early hits were toys that actually did something: the Give-A-Show projector in 1959, and in 1963 the Easy-Bake Oven, a miniature oven that genuinely baked, sold by the million and never really gone. Spirograph followed in 1966, and by 1976 a rubbery strongman called Stretch Armstrong had joined them. Kenner had a knack for turning a clever idea into a phenomenon.

Kenner Street, Cincinnati. The street that gave the company its name.
The turning point

1977: the gamble that changed everything.

When Kenner won the Star Wars license in 1977, there was no way to tool, mold and ship action figures in time for Christmas. So Kenner sold a promise instead. The Early Bird Certificate was, in effect, an empty box with a mail-away voucher. Post it back, and four figures would follow in early 1978. It should have failed. It sold in the millions.

Kenner didn't just sell toys. It invented the format the rest of the industry would spend forty years copying.

The 3¾-inch (9.5 cm) figures that arrived in 1978 set the template for action figures everywhere: small enough that a child could own a whole galaxy. Sales doubled to $200 million that year, and by 1983 more than 300 million Star Wars items had been sold. The Millennium Falcon landed in 1979, and for a while Kenner was less a toy company than a machine for manufacturing childhood.

Ones we have handled

Star Wars, on our own bench

Four Kenner figures from the 1980 Empire run came through Haarlem. Two of them carry their copyright moulded into the inside of a leg, which is the most useful thing about a Kenner figure and the thing almost no listing photographs.

Where the date lives

On the inside of the thigh, where nobody could get at it.

Kenner pressed the copyright into the tool, so it comes out on every figure that tool ever stamped, on a surface a child would never see and a reseller cannot retouch. Our 2-1B reads © L.F.L. 1980 down the left leg and MADE IN HONG KONG down the right. Our Snowtrooper reads exactly the same two things with the legs the other way round, which is a different cavity in a different tool and nothing more sinister than that.

The year on the leg is the year the tool was cut. It is not the year the figure was sold.

That distinction does the same work here as the 1989 stamp does on a Hero Turtles playset. A mould keeps stamping its own year for as long as it runs, so a figure bought in 1983 can carry 1980 in the plastic and both facts are true of different things. A listing that reports the leg as the release date is reporting the tooling and calling it the toy.

Four letters that date a figure

G.M.F.G.I. means it was tooled before Lucasfilm's name went on.

L.F.L. is Lucasfilm Ltd. The earlier figures do not say it. They say © G.M.F.G.I., for General Mills Fun Group Inc, because General Mills owned Kenner when the first wave was cut and it was General Mills whose copyright went into the steel. Reading which of the two is on a leg puts a figure on one side or the other of that change without knowing anything else about it.

The country is the other half. Hong Kong is the common one and it is what both of ours say, but the same figures were run in Taiwan, Macau and later China, and to a collector those are different objects rather than the same object from a different address. Country of origin is the most worked variant axis in the whole hobby, which is exactly why a photograph of the legs belongs in every listing and is in almost none of them.

Ask a seller for the legs. If they will not photograph the legs, you are buying the photographs they chose.

What complete means

The accessory moves the price further than the paint does.

Three figures, three answers. The Snowtrooper still holds its blue-grey rifle, so it is complete. The 2-1B has lost the tool that fitted its scanner arm, so it is not, whatever its condition says. The Ugnaught has both of the things it shipped with: the purple smock and the white tool case.

Collectors price that gap harder than they price wear. A figure with light rubbing and its original weapon clears more than a sharp one holding nothing, because the paint is what one example did over forty years and the accessory is what the release was. Wear is the figure's history. A missing accessory is a hole in the object.

Paint is what one example did over forty years. The accessory is what the release was.

Soft goods are the part that does not survive. The Ugnaught's smock is fabric, so it frays, fades and takes the dirt, and no amount of cleaning brings back a weave. It is also the piece that cannot be recut from a tool, which is why on the handful of Kenner figures that wear cloth it is the cloth and not the plastic that decides the grade.

The Real Ghostbusters, on our own bench

Five came through Haarlem: the Ecto-1, two Haunted Humans, a grabbing ghost and a Stay Puft. All five photograph their moulded stamp, all five stamps say the same company, and it is not the one that made them.

Whose name is in the plastic

Every one of them says Columbia Pictures. Not one says Kenner.

The Ecto-1 reads © 1984 COLUMBIA PICTURES, MADE IN MEXICO under the chassis. Mail Fraud carries © COLUMBIA PICTURES 1988, MADE IN CHINA across the back of his shirt. Tombstone Tackle, the same, moulded into a red thigh. Bad-To-The-Bone, 1984 and China. Stay Puft has the shortest stamp of the five, © 1984 Columbia Pictures under a foot, and that is every word the plastic offers.

The mark in the plastic is the licence, not the signature. It tells you who owned the ghost, never who moulded it.

This is the same rule a Kenner Star Wars leg follows and it catches people out in the same way. There the stamp says L.F.L., for Lucasfilm. Here it says Columbia, who made the film. In neither case does the company that cut the tool and filled it put its own name on the part. So a stamp will confirm what a toy is licensed from and where it was run, and it will not, on its own, tell you who made it. On these five, the box and the catalogue are the evidence for Kenner. The plastic is not.

A licence date, not a birthday

The Ecto-1 says 1984. It went on sale at Christmas 1987.

1984 is the film. The cartoon that this toy belongs to, The Real Ghostbusters, did not reach television until September 1986, and Kenner's line began filling Christmas orders that year. This particular Ecto-1, the cartoon version with the pop-up gun rack on the roof, was aimed at Christmas 1986 and slipped a year. The plastic is dated three years before the object existed.

That is a different error from the one a Star Wars leg makes. There the year is the tooling date and it runs a season or two behind the shelf. Here it is the film licence date, and it can be years out. What the two have in common is the direction: a moulded year is never later than the toy and is very often earlier, so a date in the plastic is a floor and never a reading.

A moulded year is a floor, not a reading. It says the toy is at least this old, and nothing about how much older.

The year does move, just not with the product. First wave carries 1984. Haunted Humans, out in 1988, carries 1988. It tracks the licence being renewed rather than the tool being cut or the toy being sold. And the addresses move too: this Ecto-1 was run in Mexico while the four figures beside it came out of China, and the Kenner Star Wars figures a decade earlier came out of Hong Kong. Three lines, three decades, three factories, one company.

What a photograph cannot tell you

On these, the mechanism is the toy. And a picture shows a toy standing still.

Every one of the four figures here does something. Tombstone Tackle is a footballer who splits open at the waist so a monster can lean out of him. Mail Fraud's shirt slides up over a set of teeth. Bad-To-The-Bone is a ribcage that closes around another figure, with a skull that hinges forward and eyes that move as it goes. Stay Puft, alone among them, is a single moulding that only stands there.

Photograph any of the three moving ones and you have proved the pieces are present and nothing about whether they still work. A hinge can be photographed shut and be split. A slide can look perfect and be seized. On a Star Wars figure the accessory is the thing that goes missing and a photograph settles it in one frame. On these, the thing that fails is invisible, and it is the reason the toy was bought in the first place.

A hinge photographs the same whether it holds or not. That is the whole problem with a listing made of photographs.

That is why our sell page asks a seller, in so many words, what the photographs cannot show. Whether it still works is a question only the person holding it can answer, and on a toy built around a mechanism it moves the price further than any amount of paint. A listing that leaves the question blank is asking you to guess, and on a Haunted Human the guess is most of what you are paying for.

Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles

Mirage Studios · Playmates Toys · 1984 – 1997

Two cartoonists, a kitchen-table joke and an idea that got wildly out of hand. In four years the Turtles went from a 3,000-copy black-and-white parody to the action figures on every shelf in the Western world.

Origins · the comic

A joke drawn at a kitchen table.

One night in November 1983, Kevin Eastman and Peter Laird were trading silly drawings in their apartment in Dover, New Hampshire. Eastman sketched a masked turtle standing upright with a pair of nunchucks, and scrawled three words above it as a gag: Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles. It was a parody, poking fun at the biggest comics of the day, Frank Miller's ninjas, the New Mutants, Dave Sim's Cerebus. It was never meant to leave the room.

But they couldn't let it go. With a tax refund and a loan from Eastman's uncle, around $1,200 between them, the pair self-published a single oversized black-and-white issue in May 1984, under an imprint they invented on the spot: Mirage Studios, so named because the studio didn't really exist. They printed 3,000 copies, priced at $1.50, and mailed out a press release that made the tiny print run sound like an event. It sold out. The gritty little comic became the most talked-about book in the independent scene, and first-printing copies now change hands for the price of a car.

The turning point

1987: color, comedy and a license to print money.

The comic was ninja-noir. The cartoon made it Saturday morning, and the toys made it a fortune.

A licensing agent named Mark Freedman saw what the comic could become, and brought it to Playmates Toys, a modest California firm with no action-figure line of its own. Playmates was interested but cautious: they wanted the Turtles on television first, to prove the audience was really there. A five-part animated miniseries aired in December 1987, and it changed everything about them.

Television softened the Turtles into the version the world remembers. In the comic all four wore red masks; on screen each got his own color so children could tell them apart. Michelangelo got the nunchucks and the one-liners, pizza became an obsession, and a whole cast, Krang, Bebop and Rocksteady, the Technodrome, was invented for the show and the toys. When Playmates shipped the first figures in 1988 the line didn't just sell. Within two years the Turtles stood shoulder to shoulder with Star Wars and G.I. Joe as the best-selling toys on Earth.

The crown jewel

The Technodrome

A foot and a half of gray plastic menace, and the toy that emptied more piggy banks than any other in the line.

If the figures were the army, the Technodrome was the empire's fortress. Released in 1990, it was Krang's rolling war-machine rendered in plastic: a giant motorized eyeball that rose from the top, prison cells, a working lift, escape hatches and a firing cannon.

It is the piece almost every collector remembers begging for, and the one that still anchors a serious Turtles collection today. Complete, boxed and unbroken, it is the blue-chip of the range.

The Sewer Playset, on our own bench

Every photograph below is of one European example that came through Haarlem, including the stamp under its base. The plastic and the carton disagree about the year, and both of them are right.

A double-decker hideout with a lift that works.

Two chambers, grey brick outside and that particular brownish orange inside, joined at the base and opened at the front so a child can reach in. Green pipes push on and pull off. A yellow post runs an elevator between the floors, and a red cable strung between two telephone poles gives you the battle swing, which is the piece every photograph of this set ends up showing.

High-Voltage Battle Swing. Real working Elevator. Realistic Sewer Pipe Passageways. Three promises, printed twice, in two languages.

The back of the carton labels the rooms like an estate agent: a Sewer Control Center, a turtle computer with a see-through screen, a fire hydrant that doubles as a periscope, and a turtle wreck-reation room, which is the sort of pun somebody was paid for in 1989 and which the German edition sensibly does not attempt.

Two dates, two objects

The mould says 1989. The box says later.

Pressed into the underside, where nobody could get at it afterwards, the tool left its mark: © 1989 Mirage Studios, Playmates Toys. That is the date worth having, and it is the one a photograph of the plastic can settle on its own. It dates the tooling.

The carton is a different object. In January 1990 the cartoon reached British television with the word ninja taken out of it: out of the title, out of the theme tune, and out of Michelangelo's hands, where the nunchucks became a grappling hook. Britain got Teenage Mutant Hero Turtles, the name carried across the European packaging, and that is why this box, printed in English and German with the Bandai mark beside the Playmates one, says Hero.

A mould goes on stamping the year it was cut, for as long as it runs.

So both dates are true and they belong to different things. The stamp says the tool was cut in 1989. The title says this carton was printed in 1990 or later, because the word on it did not exist before then. Listings collapse the two into a single year, and the year they pick is almost always the mould's, which is why a boxed set and a loose one can carry the same date and not be the same age at all.

What complete means

Sixteen parts, no figures, no Ooze.

Laid out flat it is a short list: three green pipe sections, both telephone poles and the red swing between them, the printed fence backdrop with its hole and its posters, the barricade, the manhole cover, the hydrant and its periscope, the lift post, a paving slab, and the two building halves. LJN

New York City · 1970 – 1994

The company most people can only name by its rainbow. LJN made the first E.T. toys, the rubber wrestlers that ruled the '80s playground, and a run of games so notorious the logo became a punchline. Behind those three letters is a stranger story.

Origins

Named after the boss, backwards.

LJN was founded in New York in 1970 by Jack Friedman, and the three letters have puzzled collectors for decades. They aren't his initials. In the 1960s Friedman worked as a sales rep for a toy man named Norman J. Lewis. When he struck out on his own, he named the company after his old boss with the name reversed: Lewis J. Norman, L.J.N. It has been hiding on every box the whole time.

Friedman had a nose for a license nobody else wanted. In 1982, when Steven Spielberg's E.T. was still an unknown quantity and the big toymakers were nervous, LJN took the film rights for a reported $35,000, pocket change even then, and produced the first-ever E.T. toys. It set the pattern for everything that followed: LJN would buy the license others were too cautious to touch.

The secret history

The toys were beloved. The games were infamous.

In 1984 LJN won the license for the World Wrestling Federation, and the Wrestling Superstars line changed the schoolyard. At eight inches these were giants, molded in solid rubber so they survived any beating a child could hand out. Hulk Hogan, Andre the Giant, Roddy Piper, a whole roster you could actually throw across a room. For Thundercats a year later, LJN built its clever 'Battle-Matic' spring action into the figures. For a while it could do no wrong.

To a collector the rainbow logo means treasure. To a gamer it means a warning.

Then came the other half of the story. MCA/Universal bought LJN in 1985 for around $66 million, and in 1987 the company began publishing games for the Nintendo Entertainment System. Here is the part few remember: LJN never actually made a single game. It only put its rainbow logo on titles built by others, Friday the 13th, A Nightmare on Elm Street, Back to the Future, many of them so poor that decades later the logo became internet shorthand for a bad game. Same three letters, two opposite reputations.

Origins

A wind-up chimp, and a son who dropped out.

Lewis and Barbara Galoob started their company in San Francisco in 1957, selling toys and stationery out of a modest operation. Their first real hit is one you have almost certainly seen even if you don't know the name: a battery-powered Jolly Chimp that banged a pair of cymbals and grinned. That same wide-eyed monkey later became horror-film shorthand for dread. It kept the lights on through the 1960s.

In 1970 Lewis fell too ill to run the business, and his 21-year-old son David dropped out of the University of Southern California to take it over. It was the first of several moments when the company's survival hung by a thread. Galoob licensed television to stay afloat, and in 1983 one of those licenses was The A-Team: Mr. T, a black van and a squad of toys that rode the show straight onto every shelf.

The secret history

Saved by the smallest cars in the world.

By 1987 Galoob was in real trouble. A run of failed electronic toys and chaos in its shift to Chinese manufacturing sent sales down 40 percent and left a loss of nearly $25 million. What pulled it back was tiny: Micro Machines, launched that year, miniature vehicles with a level of detail no one expected at that scale, sold by a fast-talking pitchman who became a phenomenon in his own right.

Galoob made the Game Genie, then beat Nintendo in court for the right to sell it.

Two more secrets sit in the catalog. Galoob was behind the Game Genie, the cheat cartridge Nintendo sued to kill, and Galoob won the landmark case, establishing that letting players modify a game they owned was perfectly legal. And in 1994 it launched Sky Dancers, foam-winged dolls that flew when you pulled a cord, a smash hit that sold tens of millions before being recalled after the spinning wings caused eye injuries. By the time Hasbro bought Galoob in 1998 for $220 million, the wind-up-monkey company was the third-largest toymaker in America.

Origins

A cowboy, a spaceman, and a film nobody had seen.

In January 1995, Albert Chan of Thinkway Toys was at the Toronto Toy Fair when Disney's Canadian licensing team approached him with an unusual proposition. There was an animated film coming that November. Not drawn animation. Computer animation.

Chan was shown early artwork of two characters: a cowboy named Woody and a space ranger named Buzz Lightyear. Disney wanted to know whether Thinkway was interested in making the toys.

It was a much bigger gamble than it sounds today. Toy Story would be Pixar's first feature and the first feature-length film created entirely with computer animation. There was no precedent for it, and nobody knew whether an audience would sit with characters that looked like this for ninety minutes.

And there was another problem. Time. Toy companies normally wanted well over a year to develop a major film line. Thinkway had months, because Disney was shopping a Thanksgiving 1995 release at the beginning of that same year.

Mattel had passed. Hasbro had passed. Thinkway said yes.

The company went to New York the following month, presented its ideas to Disney's American team and secured the master toy licence for Toy Story. By August, Woody and Buzz were real toys. By November, the world knew exactly who they were.

Thinkway · Toy Story
The biggest toy companies saw a risk. Thinkway saw two great toys.
The other gamble

While Thinkway bet on the toys, Steve Jobs was betting on Pixar.

Toy Story itself existed because another unlikely bet had been running for almost a decade. In 1986, shortly after leaving Apple, Steve Jobs bought George Lucas's computer graphics group and turned it into an independent company called Pixar.

For years it struggled to find a business. Pixar sold expensive graphics computers. It developed rendering software. It made commercials. And in between, a small group led creatively by John Lasseter kept making increasingly ambitious animated shorts. Jobs kept funding it.

What he was backing sounded almost as unlikely as Thinkway's licence would in 1995: that a computer could make a feature film.

Disney and Pixar agreed to make one together in 1991. That film became Toy Story. Jobs did not create Woody or Buzz, but he gave the people who did the time, the money and the company they needed to prove that computer animation could carry a whole movie.

When Toy Story opened on 22 November 1995, Pixar was still a relatively small technology company attempting something nobody had done. A week later, Jobs took it public, in the biggest flotation of the year. The experiment was over. Pixar was a film studio. And Thinkway had the toys.

The secret history

Pixar wanted Buzz to look like Buzz.

There was one particularly important instruction from Pixar. Buzz Lightyear was not to become a conventional action figure.

In the film, Buzz believes he is a real 12-inch toy. John Lasseter wanted the toy in the shops to be 12 inches too, rather than shrunk to the five-and-a-half-inch action-figure format standard at the time. That was unusually large, and Albert Chan initially questioned whether parents would pay the higher price. Pixar insisted. Thinkway trusted them.

The result was the Ultimate Talking Buzz Lightyear: twelve inches tall, with pop-out wings, lights, electronics and phrases from the film. Beside him stood a fifteen-inch pull-string Woody.

For a child leaving the cinema, something remarkable had happened. The toys existed at almost exactly the scale they had just seen on screen. They did not look like merchandise based on Toy Story. They looked like Woody and Buzz had climbed out of it.

Thinkway · Buzz Lightyear
Pixar made a film about toys. Thinkway made the toys real.
Christmas

Then everybody wanted Buzz.

Toy Story opened at number one in America on 22 November 1995 and demand for the toys exploded. Thinkway went from being the small company willing to take a risky licence to trying to supply one of the hottest properties in the world. By that December it was selling Woody and Buzz figures in the millions, and retailers were still running out.

Buzz became particularly difficult to find. Shops sold out, parents searched from one to the next, and production could not catch demand. Thinkway had the problem every toy company dreams of and dreads at the same time: everyone wanted what it had made, and there were not enough of them.

The shortage became famous enough that Pixar turned it into a joke. In Toy Story 2, Barbie guides the toys through Al's Toy Barn past an aisle overflowing with Buzz Lightyears. The joke is that the retailers finally have plenty, a few years too late.

Origins

The first monster in vinyl.

In 1966 Japanese television was overrun by monsters. Tsuburaya Productions' Ultra Q, and then Ultraman, had launched the tokusatsu boom, and every child wanted the beasts they saw on screen. A Tokyo company called Marusan answered with something new: a Godzilla standing upright, cast in soft vinyl, bright and light and cheap enough to end up in every toy box. It was the first kaiju soft-vinyl figure, and it started a genre.

The method gave each one its character. Liquid vinyl was poured into a heated metal mold, slushed around to coat the inside, then pulled out by hand and cooled. Every figure was sprayed by hand, so no two came out exactly alike. Marusan had invented sofubi, the soft-vinyl monster, and for a moment it owned the shelves.

The secret history

The phoenix, and a fork in the road.

Marusan's triumph was brief. Within two years the toy operation had collapsed into bankruptcy. But the story didn't end there: in 1969 its former staff regrouped as Bullmark, inherited many of Marusan's molds, and carried the genre into its golden age. Through the early 1970s Bullmark was the name in kaiju vinyl, Godzilla, Ultraman's rogues, Kamen Rider's villains, until the 1977 oil crisis took it under too.

Marusan invented the monster. Bullmark perfected it. Popy quietly built the empire that outlived them both.

The third house took a different road. Popy, founded in 1971 as an arm of Bandai, made its own kaiju but poured its real energy into die-cast: the Chogokin super-robots, starting with Mazinger Z. That heavy metal line became the foundation of Bandai's toy empire and the ancestor of the collectibles Bandai still sells today. Of the three houses, the one that survived was the one that changed the material. These were domestic toys, barely exported, which is exactly why a hand-sprayed Bullmark in a rare colorway now sells for the price of a car.

Sofubi

Soft vinyl · Tokyo · 1953 –

Not a company and not a character. Sofubi is a material and a way of working: hollow Japanese toys slush-cast from soft vinyl in heated metal moulds, pulled out of those moulds by hand, and sprayed by hand afterwards. Seventy years on, the method has barely changed, and the last stretch of it is still a person with an airbrush deciding what colour a monster is.

Origins

A ban in Washington, a material in Tokyo.

Sofubi is a contraction of sofuto biniiru, soft vinyl, and the reason Japan has a word for it at all is a piece of American safety law. In 1953 the United States banned flammable celluloid in consumer products. Japanese toy companies had built their export business on celluloid, and almost overnight they had to find something else to pour. They found PVC, soft and cheap and bright, and in the same year Masudaya put out what is generally held to be the first Japanese soft-vinyl doll: a baby that drank milk.

The monsters came later. When Ultra Q and Ultraman set off the tokusatsu boom in 1966, Marusan answered with a Godzilla cast in this new material, and the kaiju figure was born. That story has a page of its own. This one is about the stuff itself, and about the hands it passes through on the way to a shelf.

A figure pulled from the mould
Every piece comes out of its mould by hand, with tongs, one at a time. It is the step that has resisted automation for sixty years.
The method

Poured, cooked, and pulled out by hand.

Rotocasting, also called slush casting. It is the same sequence Maruyama Toys was running in Katsushika sixty years ago, in a workshop in a quiet residential street, and it is the reason a sofubi figure is hollow and light and never quite identical to the one beside it.

  1. WaxA sculptor works the figure in wax, and the metal mould is grown off that prototype by electroplating.
  2. PourLiquid soft vinyl goes into the mould, sometimes mixed with glow powder before it does.
  3. VacuumInto a vacuum chamber, to pull the air bubbles out of the liquid.
  4. 200°CThe moulds go into a heated chemical bath until the vinyl against the wall has set.
  5. DrainThe vinyl that has not set is poured back out and used again. This is what leaves the figure hollow.
  6. CoolA water bath, and then the part is pulled from the mould by hand with heavy tongs.
  7. FinishTrimmed, assembled on joints, and sent on to be painted.
Around eight separate companies touch a single figure: the sculptor, the mould maker, the plater, the joint maker, the vinyl producer, the factory, the colourist, the assembler.
The last step

Copper masks and a steady hand.

A blank pull is a single colour. Everything after that is spray, and the masks that shape it.

Detail is laid down through thin copper paint masks, cut to the shape of a stripe or a jaw or an eye and held against the figure while the colour goes on. A simple toy might need one or two. A complicated one can take more than a dozen, each registered against the last, each a separate pass.

Between and around the masks, the work is freehand airbrush. That is the whole explanation for something collectors notice constantly and rarely account for: two figures out of the same mould, same factory, same week, will not match. The vinyl is identical. The hand is not.

Which is also why, in a trade with this many companies in the chain, the name people actually learn is the painter's.

The painter

Hiroshi Goto, who has been colouring sofubi for seventy years.

Behind a house in Yotsugi, in Tokyo's Katsushika ward, there is a small weathered building with paint cans along the wall, unpainted figures stacked in boxes, a counter to work at and a chair for whoever has come to collect. The roof leaks. Goto Hiroshi has mentioned fixing it, and then mentioned the rain again, and gone on working. This is the Goto Coloring Workshop. Asked how many soft-vinyl toys he has painted, he says he lost count past a million.

He was born around 1937 and he did not come to this sideways. His father ran a celluloid toy factory and hand-painted the output himself, and Goto grew up in that workshop and learned to mix colour there as a boy. Then the celluloid ban came, the material changed under the whole industry, and the son carried his father's trade across to the thing that replaced it. He has been painting soft vinyl ever since, which by now is more than seventy years.

The collecting trade has it that he started as a teenager at Marusan, the company that cast the first kaiju in vinyl and whose story is on the kaiju page. We have found that in one place and not two, so treat it as the trade's account rather than a fact we can stand behind. What is not in doubt is that he was there at the start and is still at it.

Collectors in America and Europe know him as Master Goto. He does sign: H. GOTO, brushed by hand across the back of a piece. But that is not how the work is usually known. It is known by the colour, and people who have handled enough of it name it before they turn the figure over.

Goto Coloring Workshop
Goto Hiroshi at the counter
He mixes to order from dozens of pigments, the way a bartender builds a drink. Clients send figures from the United States, China and Mexico to be painted in this shed.
Goto colors

What he does that nobody else does.

Two things, and only one of them is a technique.

The first is mixing. His gradations are built out of dozens of blended pigments, and people who have handled enough of his work can name it on sight without being told. The trade calls them Goto colors, and the name does more work than the signature does.

The second is the shadows. Once a figure is painted and finished, he sprays the whole of it with a dark paint of his own formulation and immediately wipes it back off. What stays behind sits in the recesses, and the figure gains depth it did not have a moment earlier. The reason nobody else does this is the paint: his lifts off cleanly without taking the colours underneath with it, and it dries fast enough that the whole move happens in seconds. Both halves of that are his own recipe.

He works with the Tokyo shop One Up, in Nakano Broadway and Akihabara, and with sofubi builders including Picopico and Gum Taro. In 2019 Volkswagen Japan built a brand film around him and released two original kaiju he painted, Tanger and Okiterun, a hundred hand-painted of each. In 2020 TV Tokyo turned up in Yotsugi to film him for a programme about the neighbourhood.

Katsushika was once full of coloring workshops. He is one of the last of them still open.

There is a version of this page that treats that as sad. It is not quite the right note. The work is not preserved, it is simply still being done: a man in his late eighties, at a counter, under a roof he has repaired, painting monsters that will be shipped to Mexico.

After the boom

How it came back.

The kaiju boom faded in the mid-1970s and took the toys with it. What revived sofubi was not nostalgia for monsters. It was fashion.

Through the 1980s the old figures became collectable, which kept the knowledge alive without making anything new. Then in the 1990s the Ura-Harajuku street labels started commissioning soft vinyl as part of their own world, and the designer sofubi era began: same material, same moulds, same workshops, but short runs made by named people rather than long runs made by a corporation.

Medicom opened in 1996. In 2014 it launched Vinyl Artist Gacha, five-hundred-yen capsule toys made with working artists, which put the form in front of an audience that had never heard of Bullmark. Around it a genuinely international set of makers grew up: Ayako Takagi, who developed Uamou while studying at Camberwell in London and now runs a shop near Akihabara; Cory Privitera of Science Patrol; Candie Bolton in Los Angeles, whose Bakekujira took the 2017 Designer Toy Award for soft vinyl; Aaron Wilkes of Sqdblstr, working out of GeGeGe no Kitaro and tsukumogami folklore; Remjie Malham; and Awesome Toy in Hong Kong.

What separates their work from a Showa shelf toy is not the process, which is unchanged, but the run. These are made in small numbers by individuals, often with something in the vinyl itself: glitter suspended in the pour, or phosphorescent powder so the figure holds light after dark. The figure is still hollow, still pulled by hand, still sprayed by somebody.

Imperial Toy

The company that proved a toy did not need a television commercial, a giant box or a place on the Christmas list to become unforgettable. Origins

A second childhood, built in rubber and plastic.

Fred Kort arrived in the United States in 1947 with almost nothing. Born in Germany in 1923, he had survived persecution, forced labour and imprisonment at Treblinka during the Holocaust. After the war he trained and worked as an electrician, eventually moving to California. A chance repair job at Los Angeles' Biltmore Hotel introduced him to a guest working in the toy industry. Kort took a job with him and learned the business.

Twenty years later, he was ready to build something of his own.

In 1969 Kort and the young salesman Arnie Rubin founded Imperial Toy in Los Angeles. Their first hit was gloriously simple: a small high-bounce ball. It sold for around ten cents. Then came bubbles, rubber animals and the inexpensive rack toys that would become Imperial's speciality.

Kort later wondered whether his attraction to toys was connected to the childhood he had lost. If so, Imperial became quite a way of getting it back.

The little Los Angeles company grew into an international toy business producing hundreds of different products and selling in more than eighty countries. At its height, thousands of people worked for Imperial. Kort also used his success far beyond the toy industry, supporting Holocaust remembrance, humanitarian organisations, universities and medical institutions, and becoming one of the founders of the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum.

His life had begun in one world. Imperial was the world he chose to build afterward.

The secret history

The monsters everybody could own.

Imperial understood something the major toy companies often overlooked. Not every toy needed to be an event. Some toys simply needed to be there.

Imperial lived on supermarket hooks, drugstore racks and displays near the checkout. Its toys were inexpensive, immediate and wonderfully difficult for a child to ignore. In the company's earliest years, virtually nothing cost more than a dollar.

That philosophy produced one of the strangest catalogues in American toy history. There was the Hungry Mouse in the Cheese House. The Silly Dilly Sky Diving Poopatroopers. Marvel Super Hero finger puppets. Buck Rogers Space Marbles. Jaws. Close Encounters of the Third Kind. James Bond. Hanna-Barbera characters. Bubbles in almost every imaginable form.

Imperial did not make toys feel precious. It made them accessible.

And then came the monsters. In 1985, alongside the American release of Godzilla 1985, Imperial produced its own licensed Godzilla range. The star was a wonderfully straightforward articulated vinyl Godzilla, available in several sizes and accompanied by the kind of bubble toys, bop bags and novelty products Imperial knew how to sell better than almost anyone. A year later came King Kong.

For collectors today, Japanese makers such as Marusan and Bullmark represent the exotic, beautifully made kaiju toys people dream of finding. Imperial represents something different. It made the monster you could actually take home.

Marusan and Bullmark made monsters collectors dream about. Imperial made monsters children could own.

Its Godzilla did not have to be imported from Tokyo or discovered decades later in a collector's cabinet. It could be hanging in an American drugstore, supermarket or toy shop, waiting for a child with a few dollars in their pocket. That accessibility is exactly why Imperial matters.

Thousands upon thousands of those Godzillas were bought, carried around, fought against other toys, left in gardens, thrown into toy boxes and loved until their paint disappeared. They were not made to become museum pieces. They became them anyway.

Origins

A man's surname, plus the word toy.

Alfred Edward Pallett founded the Cascelloid company in 1919, working in celluloid: combs, rattles, small moulded things. The toy side needed a name of its own, and the one he registered was his own surname with the job attached. Pallett, plus toy. Palitoy. It is printed on the back of every British Star Wars card and almost nobody reads it as what it is.

For decades the toys were a division rather than a company. Then in 1965 the toy operation at Coalville was separated out and given the Palitoy name properly, and in 1968 the parent company sold it to General Mills, the American food giant that had been quietly buying toymakers.

That sale is the reason Britain got Star Wars figures at all.

General Mills already owned Kenner and Parker Brothers. So when Kenner took the Star Wars licence in 1977 and could not make figures fast enough for the world, the British arm of the same parent company was the obvious place to send it. Palitoy did not win Star Wars in a bidding war. It inherited it through a corporate structure.

Before the Death Star

Action Man came first, and outsold everything.

In 1966 Palitoy licensed Hasbro's G.I. Joe and rebuilt him for Britain as Action Man. What arrived was not a straight copy. Over the following years he collected the details British children actually remember: the flocked hair, the gripping hands, and the eagle eyes you moved with a lever in the back of his head.

By the late 1970s more than twenty million had been sold. Whatever else Palitoy did, this was the thing it did biggest, and it is worth holding onto when the Star Wars story takes over: the company that made the cardboard Death Star was, first and for longer, the company that made Action Man.

The compromise

Kenner moulded it in plastic. Palitoy printed it on card.

Kenner's Death Star Space Station was injection-moulded plastic, three storeys of it, and it was expensive to make. Palitoy costed the same toy for the British market and the numbers did not work: the tooling and the material were beyond what the division could spend on one playset.

So it was redrawn in printed cardboard. Same idea, same gun turret and lift and trash compactor, built out of card and a few plastic parts instead of a moulded shell. It went out cheap in 1978 and it was never sold anywhere but Britain.

It was built to be affordable. It survived by being thrown away.

Which is exactly why it is now the prize. Cardboard does not forgive damp lofts, sticky tape or children, so the ones that lasted are rare in a way an injection-moulded playset never is. Complete boxed examples in serious condition trade for thousands. The cheap version outlived the expensive one, and the compromise became the most wanted thing the company ever made.

The end

The licensor took over the licensee's factory.

The design department at Coalville closed in August 1984. On the first of May 1985 most of the work stopped: of 585 people in manufacturing and distribution, 327 were made redundant. What was left of Mainline Railways went to Dapol in South Wales the same year. In 1986 the Palitoy name stopped trading.

The building did not close with it. General Mills sold its toy business, and the Coalville site carried on under Kenner Parker, staffed in part by the same people, making toys for the company whose figures Palitoy had been licensed to produce. Tonka bought Kenner Parker in 1987 for $555 million. Hasbro closed the site in 1994.

In 2017 Leicestershire County Council put a green plaque on the business park that stands there now, for a company that, in its own words, helped stoke children's imaginations. It is the only thing on the site that says what used to be made there.

Origins

A company that exists only as a stamp on a foot.

There is no origin story here, and pretending otherwise would be the easiest lie on this website. Dor Mei was a Hong Kong toymaker that cast large vinyl monsters in the shape of Godzilla without ever holding the right to do so. Who founded it, when it opened, how many people worked there, whether it made anything else at all: none of that is written down anywhere we can find.

What can be established is what the toys themselves say. The soles carry DOR MEI, a registered-trademark symbol, a country of origin and a copyright year. That is the entire archive.

That stamp is doing more work than it looks. It is the reason this page can date the figures at all, and the reason it can contradict the only encyclopaedia that mentions them. A licensed toy is documented by the licence: Toho knew exactly who was making Godzilla and on what terms, and that paperwork is why the Bandai and Marusan histories can be written at all. An unlicensed one is documented by nobody, because the whole point was not to be. The absence of a record is not an oversight. It is the business model.

Identifying one

How to know what you are holding

Three things separate a Dor Mei from the licensed figures it was pretending to be, and from the other Hong Kong casts of the same era.

  • The back plates are wrong. Godzilla's tall triangular dorsal fins are the most recognisable thing about the character, and Dor Mei did not reproduce them: our own figure carries a row of low rounded nubs down the neck that fade out along the back. Wikizilla describes these figures as having no dorsal fins at all, which is not quite what ours shows, and we are going with the toy. Either way a large vinyl kaiju whose spine is bumps rather than blades is not a licensed figure.
  • The stamp on the sole. DOR MEI with a trademark symbol, the country, and a year. Photograph it before you do anything else: on an undocumented maker the moulding is not supporting evidence, it is the only evidence.
  • Two metal studs on the head. If they are there, you have the electronic version: bridging them lights the eyes red and triggers a roar. It is a different toy commercially from the plain cast, and worth materially more when it still works, which after forty years is not a given.

Why it is here

A bootleg with its own collectors

The obvious objection: this is a knock-off, so why give it a page next to Kenner and Palitoy?

Because it stopped being a knock-off some time ago. A fake works by being mistaken for the real thing, and nobody buying a finless Dor Mei is under any illusion about what it is. Retro Brighting

Why white plastic goes yellow, and what peroxide actually does about it

A white Stormtrooper. A Ghostbusters Ecto-1. A Dreamcast. Give them enough years and they all head for the same nicotine yellow, and the forums will tell you it is the fire retardants. That is close enough to sound right and wrong enough to matter. Here is what is actually happening inside the plastic, what forty years of handling adds to it, the two recipes we use, and the step afterwards that decides whether any of it lasts.

The rot

The rubber that saves the toy is the part that fails.

ABS is three things in one name. Acrylonitrile for hardness. Styrene for stiffness and gloss. Butadiene, which is synthetic rubber, and it is the reason a Kenner figure bends instead of shattering when it goes down the stairs.

That rubber is also the weak one. It carries double bonds, which are the spare links in a molecule where something else can attach. The other two do not have them. A double bond is an open door.

Light, or just heat over enough years, knocks a hydrogen off the butadiene and leaves behind a free radical: a molecule with an unpaired electron, desperate to grab something. Oxygen obliges within moments. Then it loops. Radical, hydroperoxide, carbonyl, another radical. Every pass shortens the chain a little and lines up a few more double bonds in a row, which chemists call conjugation.

Conjugation is colour. A run of double bonds soaks up the blue end of the light, so what comes back to your eye is yellow going on brown. The restoration world names a specific culprit, 2-hydroxymuconic acid, and it may well be right. It hardly matters. The shape of the answer is not in dispute.

The yellow is not dirt sitting on the toy. It is the toy itself changing.

Which decides everything below. You cannot wash off a colour the plastic built out of its own backbone. And the same reaction that made the colour also cut the chains that made the plastic tough, so the yellowing and the brittleness are the same ageing read two ways. Everything on this page deals with the first one. Nothing here touches the second.

The additive

The flame retardants, and what everyone gets wrong

Toys and electronics moulded in ABS through the 1970s and 80s came with brominated flame retardants in the mix, because a big hollow plastic object near a heater is a fire risk and the standards said so. They are still in there. What they do is more specific than the folklore.

The mechanism is the same one that makes them useful. A flame retardant works because the bond holding the bromine is weak enough to break under heat, and the loose bromine then mops up the radicals feeding the fire. Ultraviolet light breaks that same bond at room temperature. The property that stops your toy burning is the property that will not leave it alone for forty years.

So here is the correction. Everybody repeats that the bromine turns the plastic yellow. It does not. The yellowing is the ABS oxidising, and it happens in plastics with no flame retardant in them at all.

What the bromine does is make it happen sooner and faster. It is not the pigment. Think of it as fuel thrown onto a fire that was already burning. That is a real effect and a significant one, and it is not the same claim.

The difference matters because it predicts what happens after you treat a toy. Under UV, roughly half the TBBPA and a fifth of the bromine leave the plastic inside 220 hours, and the broken-down bromine does not sit still. It travels through the polymer. Bleach the surface and there is a reservoir underneath, free to work its way out. That is the mechanism behind the most common complaint about retro brighting: it comes back.

The other layer

And then there are your hands

A toy that was played with carries something a warehouse toy never did. A film of sebum, the oil your skin makes, put there daily for years by a child who did not wash first. It is not cosmetic and it is not inert.

  • Squalene is the yellow one. It is twelve to fifteen percent of sebum and it carries six double bonds, which by now you know is an invitation. It oxidises more readily than the other oils around it, and it is the component identified as responsible for yellow staining on fabric. It does the same thing on a white toy. Nobody launders the toy.
  • Once it oxidises it starts feeding the reaction. Squalene does not just sit there being yellow. It breaks down into radicals, on the surface of a plastic whose whole failure mode is radical attack. The fingerprint joins in, working from the outside in.
  • The fatty acids act as solvents. They are close to sixty percent of sebum. They swell ABS slightly, wash the protective additives out of the surface layer, and crack a moulding that was left under tension. On soft PVC, a cape or a vinyl kaiju, they speed up plasticiser migration. That is the tacky, greasy figure every collector has met.
It explains something you have probably noticed. Yellowing on a played-with figure is often patchy rather than even, and sometimes the pattern reads as a grip. That is two things at once: the bulk oxidation running everywhere, and a local dose of oxidiser and solvent left exactly where a hand held it. Sometimes you can almost see where they were held.

Wash it first. Always. Lukewarm water, a drop of pH-neutral detergent, a soft brush, dried properly and not left damp in a seam. That is it.

Do that and you have removed a fuel source, not treated a symptom. It is cheap, it is safe, and you can undo it by doing nothing. Removing forty years of skin oil is cleaning. Everything after this line is intervention.

The chemistry of the fix

Peroxide bleaches. It does not repair.

Hydrogen peroxide attacks those lined-up double bonds. Break the run and it stops absorbing blue light, so your eye stops seeing yellow. Same trick as taking the colour out of a tomato stain. The molecule is still there. Its ability to show a colour is gone.

So the plastic looks younger. Chemically, it is not younger.

The UV light is not decoration. It splits the peroxide into hydroxyl radicals, which is what makes this take hours instead of geological time. The oxy laundry booster in every recipe is sodium percarbonate with TAED, which reacts with peroxide to make peracetic acid, a sharper oxidant that works at room temperature.

Peroxide plus that booster plus UV is the whole of what people mean by retro brighting. It was worked out on the English Amiga Board over the summer of 2008, after the CBM Museum Wuppertal published a five-day soak that March. Peroxide on yellowed household plastic goes back to the 1960s as a home remedy.

What it cannot do is anything structural. It does not rebuild the butadiene. It does not reverse the chain scission, the cutting of the molecular chains that made the plastic tough. It does nothing about the brittleness, which is the half of the damage that actually threatens the object.

A retrobrighted toy is a bleached toy, not a restored polymer. It is exactly as degraded as it was and now looks like it is not. Hold that in mind and every decision below gets easier.

If the mechanism above is right, the glazing yellows first and the white holds longer. If they go at the same rate, the protectant is doing less than it claims. Either answer is worth having. The only cost is photographing this car again in a year, same angles, same light.

And we took a good sticker off on purpose. This one arrived as three states at once. The tailgate badge was already gone. One door carried a replacement somebody had fitted in the wrong size. The other door still had a sound original.

Treat that and you get a clean white car still wearing three states, which reads as a muddle rather than a history. So they all came off, the good one included, and it wears new ones. The number plates stayed, and they are the only original markings left on it.

That is the judgement the rule further down leaves you with. Never take peroxide to a sticker settles what you must not do. It does not settle what to do with stickers that were already past saving before you started. Here the answer was to make it one honest state instead of three, and then to say so out loud.

What it is. A PAL Super Nintendo, model SNSP‑001A, with SNSP‑HOL on the plate: the Dutch machine. Japan had it as the Super Famicom from November 1990 and America from September 1991, but Europe waited. Britain got it in April 1992 and the rest of the continent that June, and the underside of this one is stamped 1992. It is a first-year console, thirty-four years old, and it was bought here.

There is nothing rare about it. Nintendo sold 49 million of them worldwide and kept the European ones in production until 1998. What is rare is a clean one, and that is entirely a story about plastic.

Why this machine in particular. The shell is ABS loaded with a brominated flame retardant, which the fire-safety rules of the period required. That bromine is the accelerant named at the top of this page: it does not start the reaction, it speeds up the one the ultraviolet begins. It is the reason a console from 1992 browns in a way a toy of the same age often does not.

Which is also why it browns unevenly. The dose was not constant. Nintendo adjusted it over the production run, and a console is assembled from mouldings that need not have come from the same batch, so two parts that spent the same decades in the same living room can sit a shade apart. Add the obvious: the top of a console faces the room and the underside faces the carpet.

Both of those are in the first photograph. The upper shell is the darkest thing in the frame, the small mouldings beside it are paler, and the cartridge guide that never saw daylight is still white.

The evidence against

Ten years later, the treated side looked worse

Anyone thinking about doing this should see this experiment first.

In 2015 part of a Dreamcast shell was masked off with tape while the rest was retrobrighted. Treated and untreated ABS then sat side by side on the same object, in the same room, under the same light, for ten years.

It was compared again in 2025. The treated areas had yellowed more than the untreated ones beside them, and carried streaking and blotching the masked areas did not. Not slower. Not equal. Worse.

That is one Dreamcast shell, not a controlled study, and it deserves to be called that. But as hobby experiments go it is unusually good, because the sample and the control are the same piece of plastic. And what happened makes chemical sense. The bleaching removed the colour near the surface. It did not remove the degraded material underneath, it did not repair the broken chains, and it did nothing to stop light, heat and oxygen carrying on.

Conservation science has no reversal to offer either. The Getty runs a plastics programme precisely because the field does not yet know how to save these objects, and nothing in it endorses bleaching them. We can take the colour off. We cannot repair the polymer. That is not going to change.

But look at what that shell did next. It went back on a shelf with nothing at all between it and the light that had yellowed it the first time. A decade of the original cause, on a surface with less left to lose.

So this is not an argument against retrobrighting. It is an argument against retrobrighting something and then putting it straight back on the same sunny shelf, which is what almost every published before-and-after actually shows.

After the bleach

The part people tend to forget

Bleaching removes the visible result of the oxidation. It does nothing to the oxidation. So once a part comes out of the gel, the only question left is what you put between it and the thing that started all of this.

Read the chain backwards and there are three things driving it. Light and heat get it started. Oxygen keeps it going. The bromine coming off the flame retardant speeds it up. Peroxide addresses none of them. It removes what they produced.

Of those three, exactly one is under your control on a shelf. You cannot take the oxygen away. You certainly cannot take the flame retardant out of something moulded in 1984. You can take the light away.

That is why we use a UV protectant afterwards. We currently use 303 Aerospace Protectant, and the reasons are as much about what is not in the bottle as what is.

Our expectation is simply that a protected piece should yellow more slowly than the same piece under the same light without it. That is a prediction, not a result, because the long-term data does not exist yet.

Which is why we write down what was done to every treated toy we sell, and when. In ten years that record will be worth more than any before-and-after photograph.

Our position

Where we draw the line

We do retrobright toys. A good part of the shelf has been through it. But there are places we stop, and none of them are negotiable for a better-looking photograph.

  • Never over paint, tampo printing, stickers or glue. Peroxide lifts printing, dulls paint, softens adhesive and finds the edge of a sticker. Losing an original factory marking is a far bigger problem than yellow plastic, because that marking is often what dates the piece.
  • Never a multi-material toy as one job. A head, torso and arms can look identical and be different plastic from different tools. They bleach at different speeds. That is how one yellow figure becomes one white, one cream and one grey figure.
  • Never when the ageing is what identifies the object. On a bootleg, an undocumented cast or an odd variant, the surface itself is part of the evidence. Take it off and it cannot be put back, on the one kind of object where the object is the whole archive.
  • Never without saying so. Every treated toy we sell is described as treated, alongside what was used. A buyer should know whether the white they are looking at is forty years old, or forty years old and recently bleached.

And there is a fifth, which is not a rule so much as a habit. If the yellowing is even, the paint and printing are intact and the thing simply looks its age, we usually leave it alone.

Age is not automatically damage that needs fixing.

The short version

If you remember nothing else

Wash it first. Lukewarm water, pH-neutral detergent, soft brush, dried properly. That alone removes something that is still working on the plastic.

Then decide whether it needs treating at all. A great deal of vintage plastic looks perfectly good with even, honest ageing, and doing nothing is a real answer.

If you do treat it: the lowest concentration that will do the job. Check it far more often than feels necessary. Stop when it matches the original plastic around it, not when it reaches refrigerator white. Rinse it properly.

Then protect it from UV, because an unprotected bleached surface is the Dreamcast. Store it cool, dry, out of direct sun, off fluorescent tubes, and not sealed in a PVC bag.

And write down what you did. Somebody looking at that toy in ten years deserves to know what happened to it.

Moulds

How a toy is actually made, and how to read which way from the toy itself

Almost every object in this shop started as a cavity cut into a block of steel. Four different processes filled those cavities, and each one leaves its own evidence on the finished piece: a seam, a circle, a thickened ridge, a line of raised text on the back of a leg. None of it is damage. It is the manufacturing record, and once you can read it you can tell how a toy was made, often where, and usually when.

Why it is worth knowing

The marks are not flaws.

A collector learns early to look for a seam down the side of a figure and a ring of faint circles on its back, and a collector who has not been told what those are tends to read them as wear, or as a sign of a cheap copy. They are neither. They are the places where the tool opened and where the part was pushed off it, and they are present on the finest Kenner figure ever made.

What they are worth is information. The process tells you what the thing is: a hollow soft-vinyl monster and a hard styrene action figure are different objects made in different buildings, and they age differently, clean differently and break differently. And the one piece of text that is unique to your particular toy, the moulded stamp, is cut into the tool itself. It is the closest thing a mass-produced object has to a birth certificate.

Process one

Injection moulding

Hard plastic, sharp detail, enormous numbers. Nearly every action figure, vehicle and playset wall in this shop came out of a steel injection tool.

A tool is two halves. The cavity cuts the outside of the part and the core forms the inside, both machined out of tool steel, typically P20, H13, S7 or 420 stainless. Molten plastic is forced in through a tapered sprue, along a runner to each cavity, and through a gate into the part itself. Water runs through drilled channels to cool it. Air escapes through vents ground to less than 0.02 mm, small enough that plastic will not follow it out.

Then the halves separate and ejector pins push the part off the core. A rectangular part needs at least four, one at each corner. A cylindrical one needs three at 120 degrees. A sleeve can do the job instead and leaves nothing behind, which is why some parts have no pin marks at all.

One tool usually holds several cavities, and one shot fills all of them in one colour. That is the whole reason a playset arrives as a bag of brown railings, a bag of red ladders and a bag of grey walls: those are not assortments, they are shots.

Process two

Rotational and slush casting

Soft, hollow, light, and warm to the touch. Vinyl monsters, squeeze toys, dolls' heads and limbs.

Here the mould is not injected but filled and tipped. Liquid vinyl goes into a heated metal mould, the mould is turned so the liquid coats the inside, and the surplus is poured back out. What sets against the wall is the toy. What drains away is used again, which is exactly why the figure is hollow.

There are no ejector pins because nothing is pushed off a core: the part is pulled out of the mould by hand, often through its own opening, which is why a rotocast figure has a hole somewhere you are not meant to look. There is a seam, but a soft one. And because the final colour is sprayed on afterwards rather than mixed into the shot, two figures from the same mould genuinely do not match.

Process three

Blow moulding

Big hollow things. Ride-on toys, bats, bowling pins, the larger vehicle bodies, anything that had to be light and cheap and enormous.

A soft tube of hot plastic, a parison, is hung between the two halves of a mould. The mould closes on it, pinching it shut at the ends, and air is blown in until the tube balloons against the walls. It is the same thing that makes a milk bottle, scaled up and given a face.

It leaves a tell that nothing else does. Where the mould pinched the tube closed, the material is folded on itself, so the seam is thicker than the wall around it rather than thinner. Usually it runs along the bottom or round the back where it will not be looked at, and on a cheaper piece it was never trimmed off at all.

Look for: a ridge you can feel with a fingernail, fatter than the plastic either side of it, on a part that is large, light and hollow.
Process four

Vacuum forming

The cheapest way to get a shape, and the reason so much of a playset is scenery rather than structure.

A flat sheet of plastic is heated until it sags, laid over a tool, and the air underneath is sucked out so the sheet is pulled down onto the shape. No second half, no injection, no pressure to speak of. Minutes of work and a tool that can be made of wood or resin instead of hardened steel.

You can read it instantly once you know: the inside is a perfect negative of the outside, because there is only one surface. It thins at the corners, where the sheet had to stretch furthest, so deep detail goes pale and slightly translucent. And there is no parting line anywhere, because the mould never closed on anything.

Playset terrain, the trays inside a carded blister, and the blister itself are all this process.

And the one that is not plastic

Die casting

The same idea in metal, and the reason a Matchbox car feels like it does.

Molten zinc alloy, usually a zamak, is forced under pressure into a hardened steel die. The sequence is injection moulding exactly, with a melt that happens to be metal: two halves, a gate, ejector pins, a parting line. The difference is in the hand. It is heavy, it holds a crisper edge than any plastic will, and where it has been dropped it dents instead of cracking.

In this trade that means Matchbox and Hot Wheels bodies, and in Japan it means Chogokin, the die-cast super-robots Popy built Bandai's business on.

Look for: weight out of all proportion to size, a seam that is metal rather than plastic, and chips that show bright grey underneath the paint.
In the hand

How to read a toy in thirty seconds

Squeeze it, tap it, turn it over, find the seam. Four questions and you have the process.

  1. SqueezeGives under the thumb? Rotocast vinyl. Rigid? Keep going.
  2. WeighHeavy for its size, dents rather than cracks? Die-cast.
  3. SeamA crisp line round the silhouette with small circles nearby? Injection. A thick folded ridge on a big hollow part? Blow moulded. No seam, one shell, pale at the corners? Vacuum formed.
  4. Turn itNow read the stamp: maker, country, and a year that is the year of the tool, not the year of the film.

That last one is the one worth the most. A moulded date is cut into the steel, so it dates the tool rather than the production run, and a tool stays in service for years. It is why a figure stamped 1980 can have left the factory in 1983, and why we date from the stamp and say so on every lot. If a stamp is worn to the point of being unreadable, that is itself a fact about the tool: steel wears, and the later the pull, the softer the letters.

What a mark is not is a reason to discount a piece. If a listing of ours calls out a seam, a pin circle or a gate dot, that is a description of how it was made. The things we do grade down are in condition and surface, which is a different page.

The four zones

Shipping and customs do not follow the same map, which is where most people go wrong. Europe is not the EU. The UK, Switzerland and Norway are geographically European but outside the EU customs union, so purchases from them may still be imports. For shipping, we use four zones:

  • Netherlands Domestic shipping. No customs.
  • EU EU member states. No import VAT or customs duty between member states.
  • UK / Europe outside the EU Nearby for shipping, but treated as imports for customs.
  • Rest of world All other countries, including the US and Japan.
