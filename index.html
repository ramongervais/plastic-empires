The story

Kenner sold an empty box, and started an empire.

In 1977, Kenner had the kind of problem most companies only dream about. Star Wars had become the biggest film on Earth, every child wanted the toys, and Kenner had almost nothing on the shelf. There was no way to tool, mold and ship figures in time for Christmas.

So they sold a promise. The Early Bird Certificate Package was, essentially, an empty box with a cardboard diorama and a mail-away voucher. Post it back, and sometime in early 1978 four action figures would arrive: Luke, Leia, R2-D2 and Chewbacca. It should have been a disaster. Instead it sold in the millions.

They wrapped up an empty box for Christmas. Children unwrapped a promise, and waited for the post.

The figures that followed in 1978 invented a format the whole industry would copy: the 3¾-inch action figure, small enough that a child could own a galaxy. Vehicles came next. But the one everybody wanted, the flagship itself, was held back.

The Millennium Falcon did not arrive until 1979. When it did, it was enormous for its day: a light-up cockpit, a spinning radar dish, a training remote, and the little chess table from the film. It was the toy you asked for and rarely got. Forty-odd years on, a complete one is a small monument to how much a plastic ship could mean.

Origins

Named after a street. Built to last.

In 1947, three brothers, Albert, Philip and Joseph Steiner, started a toy company in Cincinnati, Ohio, and named it after Kenner Street, where their office stood. It was a small beginning for a firm that would end up shaping the shelves of nearly every toy shop in the Western world.

Their early hits were toys that actually did something: the Give-A-Show projector in 1959, and in 1963 the Easy-Bake Oven, a miniature oven that genuinely baked, sold by the million and never really gone. Spirograph followed in 1966, and by 1976 a rubbery strongman called Stretch Armstrong had joined them. Kenner had a knack for turning a clever idea into a phenomenon.

Kenner Street, Cincinnati. The street that gave the company its name.
The turning point

1977: the gamble that changed everything.

When Kenner won the Star Wars license in 1977, there was no way to tool, mold and ship action figures in time for Christmas. So Kenner sold a promise instead. The Early Bird Certificate was, in effect, an empty box with a mail-away voucher. Post it back, and four figures would follow in early 1978. It should have failed. It sold in the millions.

Kenner didn't just sell toys. It invented the format the rest of the industry would spend forty years copying.

The 3¾-inch (9.5 cm) figures that arrived in 1978 set the template for action figures everywhere: small enough that a child could own a whole galaxy. Sales doubled to $200 million that year, and by 1983 more than 300 million Star Wars items had been sold. The Millennium Falcon landed in 1979, and for a while Kenner was less a toy company than a machine for manufacturing childhood.

Origins · the comic

A joke drawn at a kitchen table.

One night in November 1983, Kevin Eastman and Peter Laird were trading silly drawings in their apartment in Dover, New Hampshire. Eastman sketched a masked turtle standing upright with a pair of nunchucks, and scrawled three words above it as a gag: Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles. It was a parody, poking fun at the biggest comics of the day, Frank Miller's ninjas, the New Mutants, Dave Sim's Cerebus. It was never meant to leave the room.

But they couldn't let it go. With a tax refund and a loan from Eastman's uncle, around $1,200 between them, the pair self-published a single oversized black-and-white issue in May 1984, under an imprint they invented on the spot: Mirage Studios, so named because the studio didn't really exist. They printed 3,000 copies, priced at $1.50, and mailed out a press release that made the tiny print run sound like an event. It sold out. The gritty little comic became the most talked-about book in the independent scene, and first-printing copies now change hands for the price of a car.

The turning point

1987: color, comedy and a license to print money.

A licensing agent named Mark Freedman saw what the comic could become, and brought it to Playmates Toys, a modest California firm with no action-figure line of its own. Playmates was interested but cautious: they wanted the Turtles on television first, to prove the audience was really there. A five-part animated miniseries aired in December 1987, and it changed everything about them.

The comic was ninja-noir. The cartoon made it Saturday morning, and the toys made it a fortune.

Television softened the Turtles into the version the world remembers. In the comic all four wore red masks; on screen each got his own color so children could tell them apart. Michelangelo got the nunchucks and the one-liners, pizza became an obsession, and a whole cast, Krang, Bebop and Rocksteady, the Technodrome, was invented for the show and the toys. When Playmates shipped the first figures in 1988 the line didn't just sell. Within two years the Turtles stood shoulder to shoulder with Star Wars and G.I. Joe as the best-selling toys on Earth.

The crown jewel

The Technodrome

If the figures were the army, the Technodrome was the empire's fortress. Released in 1990, it was Krang's rolling war-machine rendered in plastic: a giant motorized eyeball that rose from the top, prison cells, a working lift, escape hatches and a firing cannon.

A foot and a half of gray plastic menace, and the toy that emptied more piggy banks than any other in the line.

It is the piece almost every collector remembers begging for, and the one that still anchors a serious Turtles collection today. Complete, boxed and unbroken, it is the blue-chip of the range.

New York City · 1970 – 1994

The company most people can only name by its rainbow. LJN made the first E.T. toys, the rubber wrestlers that ruled the '80s playground, and a run of games so notorious the logo became a punchline. Behind those three letters is a stranger story.

Origins

Named after the boss, backwards.

LJN was founded in New York in 1970 by Jack Friedman, and the three letters have puzzled collectors for decades. They aren't his initials. In the 1960s Friedman worked as a sales rep for a toy man named Norman J. Lewis. When he struck out on his own, he named the company after his old boss with the name reversed: Lewis J. Norman, L.J.N. It has been hiding on every box the whole time.

Friedman had a nose for a license nobody else wanted. In 1982, when Steven Spielberg's E.T. was still an unknown quantity and the big toymakers were nervous, LJN took the film rights for a reported $35,000, pocket change even then, and produced the first-ever E.T. toys. It set the pattern for everything that followed: LJN would buy the license others were too cautious to touch.

The secret history

The toys were beloved. The games were infamous.

In 1984 LJN won the license for the World Wrestling Federation, and the Wrestling Superstars line changed the schoolyard. At eight inches these were giants, molded in solid rubber so they survived any beating a child could hand out. Hulk Hogan, Andre the Giant, Roddy Piper, a whole roster you could actually throw across a room. For Thundercats a year later, LJN built its clever 'Battle-Matic' spring action into the figures. For a while it could do no wrong.

To a collector the rainbow logo means treasure. To a gamer it means a warning.

Then came the other half of the story. MCA/Universal bought LJN in 1985 for around $66 million, and in 1987 the company began publishing games for the Nintendo Entertainment System. Here is the part few remember: LJN never actually made a single game. It only put its rainbow logo on titles built by others, Friday the 13th, A Nightmare on Elm Street, Back to the Future, many of them so poor that decades later the logo became internet shorthand for a bad game. Same three letters, two opposite reputations.

Origins

A wind-up chimp, and a son who dropped out.

Lewis and Barbara Galoob started their company in San Francisco in 1957, selling toys and stationery out of a modest operation. Their first real hit is one you have almost certainly seen even if you don't know the name: a battery-powered Jolly Chimp that banged a pair of cymbals and grinned. That same wide-eyed monkey later became horror-film shorthand for dread. It kept the lights on through the 1960s.

In 1970 Lewis fell too ill to run the business, and his 21-year-old son David dropped out of the University of Southern California to take it over. It was the first of several moments when the company's survival hung by a thread. Galoob licensed television to stay afloat, and in 1983 one of those licenses was The A-Team: Mr. T, a black van and a squad of toys that rode the show straight onto every shelf.

The secret history

Saved by the smallest cars in the world.

By 1987 Galoob was in real trouble. A run of failed electronic toys and chaos in its shift to Chinese manufacturing sent sales down 40 percent and left a loss of nearly $25 million. What pulled it back was tiny: Micro Machines, launched that year, miniature vehicles with a level of detail no one expected at that scale, sold by a fast-talking pitchman who became a phenomenon in his own right.

Galoob made the Game Genie, then beat Nintendo in court for the right to sell it.

Two more secrets sit in the catalog. Galoob was behind the Game Genie, the cheat cartridge Nintendo sued to kill, and Galoob won the landmark case, establishing that letting players modify a game they owned was perfectly legal. And in 1994 it launched Sky Dancers, foam-winged dolls that flew when you pulled a cord, a smash hit that sold tens of millions before being recalled after the spinning wings caused eye injuries. By the time Hasbro bought Galoob in 1998 for $220 million, the wind-up-monkey company was the third-largest toymaker in America.

Origins

A cowboy, a spaceman, and a film nobody had seen.

In January 1995, Albert Chan of Thinkway Toys was at the Toronto Toy Fair when Disney's Canadian licensing team approached him with an unusual proposition. There was an animated film coming that November. Not drawn animation. Computer animation.

Chan was shown early artwork of two characters: a cowboy named Woody and a space ranger named Buzz Lightyear. Disney wanted to know whether Thinkway was interested in making the toys.

It was a much bigger gamble than it sounds today. Toy Story would be Pixar's first feature and the first feature-length film created entirely with computer animation. There was no precedent for it, and nobody knew whether an audience would sit with characters that looked like this for ninety minutes.

And there was another problem. Time. Toy companies normally wanted well over a year to develop a major film line. Thinkway had months, because Disney was shopping a Thanksgiving 1995 release at the beginning of that same year.

Mattel had passed. Hasbro had passed. Thinkway said yes.

The company went to New York the following month, presented its ideas to Disney's American team and secured the master toy licence for Toy Story. By August, Woody and Buzz were real toys. By November, the world knew exactly who they were.

Thinkway · Toy Story
The biggest toy companies saw a risk. Thinkway saw two great toys.
The other gamble

While Thinkway bet on the toys, Steve Jobs was betting on Pixar.

Toy Story itself existed because another unlikely bet had been running for almost a decade. In 1986, shortly after leaving Apple, Steve Jobs bought George Lucas's computer graphics group and turned it into an independent company called Pixar.

For years it struggled to find a business. Pixar sold expensive graphics computers. It developed rendering software. It made commercials. And in between, a small group led creatively by John Lasseter kept making increasingly ambitious animated shorts. Jobs kept funding it.

What he was backing sounded almost as unlikely as Thinkway's licence would in 1995: that a computer could make a feature film.

Disney and Pixar agreed to make one together in 1991. That film became Toy Story. Jobs did not create Woody or Buzz, but he gave the people who did the time, the money and the company they needed to prove that computer animation could carry a whole movie.

When Toy Story opened on 22 November 1995, Pixar was still a relatively small technology company attempting something nobody had done. A week later, Jobs took it public, in the biggest flotation of the year. The experiment was over. Pixar was a film studio. And Thinkway had the toys.

The secret history

Pixar wanted Buzz to look like Buzz.

There was one particularly important instruction from Pixar. Buzz Lightyear was not to become a conventional action figure.

In the film, Buzz believes he is a real 12-inch toy. John Lasseter wanted the toy in the shops to be 12 inches too, rather than shrunk to the five-and-a-half-inch action-figure format standard at the time. That was unusually large, and Albert Chan initially questioned whether parents would pay the higher price. Pixar insisted. Thinkway trusted them.

The result was the Ultimate Talking Buzz Lightyear: twelve inches tall, with pop-out wings, lights, electronics and phrases from the film. Beside him stood a fifteen-inch pull-string Woody.

For a child leaving the cinema, something remarkable had happened. The toys existed at almost exactly the scale they had just seen on screen. They did not look like merchandise based on Toy Story. They looked like Woody and Buzz had climbed out of it.

Thinkway · Buzz Lightyear
Pixar made a film about toys. Thinkway made the toys real.
Christmas

Then everybody wanted Buzz.

Toy Story opened at number one in America on 22 November 1995 and demand for the toys exploded. Thinkway went from being the small company willing to take a risky licence to trying to supply one of the hottest properties in the world. By that December it was selling Woody and Buzz figures in the millions, and retailers were still running out.

Buzz became particularly difficult to find. Shops sold out, parents searched from one to the next, and production could not catch demand. Thinkway had the problem every toy company dreams of and dreads at the same time: everyone wanted what it had made, and there were not enough of them.

The shortage became famous enough that Pixar turned it into a joke. In Toy Story 2, Barbie guides the toys through Al's Toy Barn past an aisle overflowing with Buzz Lightyears. The joke is that the retailers finally have plenty, a few years too late.

Origins

The first monster in vinyl.

In 1966 Japanese television was overrun by monsters. Tsuburaya Productions' Ultra Q, and then Ultraman, had launched the tokusatsu boom, and every child wanted the beasts they saw on screen. A Tokyo company called Marusan answered with something new: a Godzilla standing upright, cast in soft vinyl, bright and light and cheap enough to end up in every toy box. It was the first kaiju soft-vinyl figure, and it started a genre.

The method gave each one its character. Liquid vinyl was poured into a heated metal mold, slushed around to coat the inside, then pulled out by hand and cooled. Every figure was sprayed by hand, so no two came out exactly alike. Marusan had invented sofubi, the soft-vinyl monster, and for a moment it owned the shelves.

The secret history

The phoenix, and a fork in the road.

Marusan's triumph was brief. Within two years the toy operation had collapsed into bankruptcy. But the story didn't end there: in 1969 its former staff regrouped as Bullmark, inherited many of Marusan's molds, and carried the genre into its golden age. Through the early 1970s Bullmark was the name in kaiju vinyl, Godzilla, Ultraman's rogues, Kamen Rider's villains, until the 1977 oil crisis took it under too.

Marusan invented the monster. Bullmark perfected it. Popy quietly built the empire that outlived them both.

The third house took a different road. Popy, founded in 1971 as an arm of Bandai, made its own kaiju but poured its real energy into die-cast: the Chogokin super-robots, starting with Mazinger Z. That heavy metal line became the foundation of Bandai's toy empire and the ancestor of the collectibles Bandai still sells today. Of the three houses, the one that survived was the one that changed the material. These were domestic toys, barely exported, which is exactly why a hand-sprayed Bullmark in a rare colorway now sells for the price of a car.

Origins

A second childhood, built in rubber and plastic.

Fred Kort arrived in the United States in 1947 with almost nothing. Born in Germany in 1923, he had survived persecution, forced labour and imprisonment at Treblinka during the Holocaust. After the war he trained and worked as an electrician, eventually moving to California. A chance repair job at Los Angeles' Biltmore Hotel introduced him to a guest working in the toy industry. Kort took a job with him and learned the business.

Twenty years later, he was ready to build something of his own.

In 1969 Kort and the young salesman Arnie Rubin founded Imperial Toy in Los Angeles. Their first hit was gloriously simple: a small high-bounce ball. It sold for around ten cents. Then came bubbles, rubber animals and the inexpensive rack toys that would become Imperial's speciality.

Kort later wondered whether his attraction to toys was connected to the childhood he had lost. If so, Imperial became quite a way of getting it back.

The little Los Angeles company grew into an international toy business producing hundreds of different products and selling in more than eighty countries. At its height, thousands of people worked for Imperial. Kort also used his success far beyond the toy industry, supporting Holocaust remembrance, humanitarian organisations, universities and medical institutions, and becoming one of the founders of the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum.

His life had begun in one world. Imperial was the world he chose to build afterward.

The secret history

The monsters everybody could own.

Imperial understood something the major toy companies often overlooked. Not every toy needed to be an event. Some toys simply needed to be there.

Imperial lived on supermarket hooks, drugstore racks and displays near the checkout. Its toys were inexpensive, immediate and wonderfully difficult for a child to ignore. In the company's earliest years, virtually nothing cost more than a dollar.

That philosophy produced one of the strangest catalogues in American toy history. There was the Hungry Mouse in the Cheese House. The Silly Dilly Sky Diving Poopatroopers. Marvel Super Hero finger puppets. Buck Rogers Space Marbles. Jaws. Close Encounters of the Third Kind. James Bond. Hanna-Barbera characters. Bubbles in almost every imaginable form.

Imperial did not make toys feel precious. It made them accessible.

And then came the monsters. In 1985, alongside the American release of Godzilla 1985, Imperial produced its own licensed Godzilla range. The star was a wonderfully straightforward articulated vinyl Godzilla, available in several sizes and accompanied by the kind of bubble toys, bop bags and novelty products Imperial knew how to sell better than almost anyone. A year later came King Kong.

For collectors today, Japanese makers such as Marusan and Bullmark represent the exotic, beautifully made kaiju toys people dream of finding. Imperial represents something different. It made the monster you could actually take home.

Marusan and Bullmark made monsters collectors dream about. Imperial made monsters children could own.

Its Godzilla did not have to be imported from Tokyo or discovered decades later in a collector's cabinet. It could be hanging in an American drugstore, supermarket or toy shop, waiting for a child with a few dollars in their pocket. That accessibility is exactly why Imperial matters.

Thousands upon thousands of those Godzillas were bought, carried around, fought against other toys, left in gardens, thrown into toy boxes and loved until their paint disappeared. They were not made to become museum pieces. They became them anyway.

The most coveted Star Wars playset in the world is made of cardboard. Not because anyone wanted it that way: Palitoy could not afford the plastic. Origins

A man's surname, plus the word toy.

Alfred Edward Pallett founded the Cascelloid company in 1919, working in celluloid: combs, rattles, small moulded things. The toy side needed a name of its own, and the one he registered was his own surname with the job attached. Pallett, plus toy. Palitoy. It is printed on the back of every British Star Wars card and almost nobody reads it as what it is.

For decades the toys were a division rather than a company. Then in 1965 the toy operation at Coalville was separated out and given the Palitoy name properly, and in 1968 the parent company sold it to General Mills, the American food giant that had been quietly buying toymakers.

That sale is the reason Britain got Star Wars figures at all.

General Mills already owned Kenner and Parker Brothers. So when Kenner took the Star Wars licence in 1977 and could not make figures fast enough for the world, the British arm of the same parent company was the obvious place to send it. Palitoy did not win Star Wars in a bidding war. It inherited it through a corporate structure.

Before the Death Star

Action Man came first, and outsold everything.

In 1966 Palitoy licensed Hasbro's G.I. Joe and rebuilt him for Britain as Action Man. What arrived was not a straight copy. Over the following years he collected the details British children actually remember: the flocked hair, the gripping hands, and the eagle eyes you moved with a lever in the back of his head.

By the late 1970s more than twenty million had been sold. Whatever else Palitoy did, this was the thing it did biggest, and it is worth holding onto when the Star Wars story takes over: the company that made the cardboard Death Star was, first and for longer, the company that made Action Man.

The compromise

Kenner moulded it in plastic. Palitoy printed it on card.

Kenner's Death Star Space Station was injection-moulded plastic, three storeys of it, and it was expensive to make. Palitoy costed the same toy for the British market and the numbers did not work: the tooling and the material were beyond what the division could spend on one playset.

So it was redrawn in printed cardboard. Same idea, same gun turret and lift and trash compactor, built out of card and a few plastic parts instead of a moulded shell. It went out cheap in 1978 and it was never sold anywhere but Britain.

It was built to be affordable. It survived by being thrown away.

Which is exactly why it is now the prize. Cardboard does not forgive damp lofts, sticky tape or children, so the ones that lasted are rare in a way an injection-moulded playset never is. Complete boxed examples in serious condition trade for thousands. The cheap version outlived the expensive one, and the compromise became the most wanted thing the company ever made.

The end

The licensor took over the licensee's factory.

The design department at Coalville closed in August 1984. On the first of May 1985 most of the work stopped: of 585 people in manufacturing and distribution, 327 were made redundant. What was left of Mainline Railways went to Dapol in South Wales the same year. In 1986 the Palitoy name stopped trading.

The building did not close with it. General Mills sold its toy business, and the Coalville site carried on under Kenner Parker, staffed in part by the same people, making toys for the company whose figures Palitoy had been licensed to produce. Tonka bought Kenner Parker in 1987 for $555 million. Hasbro closed the site in 1994.

In 2017 Leicestershire County Council put a green plaque on the business park that stands there now, for a company that, in its own words, helped stoke children's imaginations. It is the only thing on the site that says what used to be made there.

What a parcel costs to send, and what a purchase from abroad really costs you.

The four zones

Shipping and customs do not follow the same map, which is where most people go wrong. Europe is not the EU. The United Kingdom, Switzerland and Norway are in Europe but sit outside the EU customs union, so a parcel from any of them is a customs import even though PostNL groups the UK into a nearby shipping zone. We therefore use four:

  • Netherlands Domestic. No customs, ever.
  • EU The 27 member states. Free circulation, so no duty and no import VAT.
  • UK / Europe non-EU United Kingdom, Switzerland, Norway. Near for shipping, foreign for customs.
  • Rest of world Everywhere else, including the US and Japan.

Buying from inside the EU

Simple. The goods are already in free circulation, so there is no duty and no import VAT to pay on arrival.

item + shipping = what you pay. A €50 figure from France with €12 postage costs you €62.

Buying from outside the EU

This is where a bargain stops being one. Four things can land on top of the item and the postage:

  • Import VAT 21% Dutch VAT, charged on the customs value, which includes the shipping.
  • Customs duty Since 1 July 2026 the EU has abolished the old €150 duty relief. A flat €3 per goods category applies to consignments of €150 or less, an interim measure running to 1 July 2028. Above €150, normal duty applies according to the commodity code.
  • Handling The carrier charges its own clearance fee. A further €2 per consignment EU handling fee is due in November 2026.
  • The exchange rate Whatever your card issuer decides to add.

item + shipping + import VAT + duty + handling = landed cost. A €100 Godzilla from Japan with €23 postage lands nearer €155 than €123.

Selling from the Netherlands

Your buyer pays the item plus the shipping shown on the lot, in one payment. If they are outside the EU their own country may then charge them import VAT, duty and handling on arrival, which is theirs to pay and not something we can quote for them. Business exports from the Netherlands can be zero-rated for Dutch VAT where the conditions are met, which is a question for an accountant rather than for us.
